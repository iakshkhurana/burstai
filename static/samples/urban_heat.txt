Why our city feels hotter at night

Urban heat islands form when cities replace natural land cover with dense concentrations of pavement, buildings, and other surfaces that absorb and retain heat. This effect increases energy costs, air pollution levels, and heat-related illness and mortality.

Walking home in July, I noticed the asphalt outside our building was still warm at midnight. Moreover, it is important to note that the multifaceted nature of urban heat necessitates a comprehensive and holistic approach to mitigation. Ultimately, cities must leverage innovative strategies to foster resilient, thriving communities.

Planting street trees is the cheapest fix we found. Shade lowers surface temperatures and the leaves cool the air as they release water. The heat effects people who cannot afford air conditioning the most, so their is also a case for cooling centres in every ward.

Our survey of twelve streets found the shaded ones was noticeably cooler by late evening, and the residents we spoke to said the same.
