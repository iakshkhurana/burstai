The lighthouse keeper had not spoken to another person in three years, and he had come to prefer it that way. Each morning, he climbed the spiral staircase, polished the great lens until it gleamed, and watched the gray waves roll endlessly toward the rocks below. The solitude was not loneliness, he told himself. It was peace.

Then, one storm-tossed evening in late October, a small wooden boat appeared on the horizon, pitching violently against the swells. Through his telescope, he could make out a single figure huddled at the stern, clinging desperately to the tiller. His heart pounded as he realized the vessel was heading straight for the jagged shoals that had claimed so many ships before.

Without hesitation, he lit the signal fire and rang the brass bell, its mournful tone cutting through the howling wind. The boat veered, narrowly avoiding the rocks, and drifted toward the sheltered cove beneath the lighthouse. He raced down the stairs, lantern in hand, and waded into the frigid surf to pull the stranger ashore.

She was young, perhaps twenty, with dark hair plastered to her pale face and eyes that held a quiet determination. "Thank you," she whispered, her voice barely audible above the storm. He wrapped her in his coat and led her inside, where the fire crackled warmly.

As the night wore on and the tempest raged outside, they sat in comfortable silence. For the first time in three years, the lighthouse keeper felt something stir within him, a feeling he had long thought extinguished. Perhaps, he mused, solitude was not the same as peace after all.
