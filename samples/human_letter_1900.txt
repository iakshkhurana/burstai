My dear Alice,

Your letter reached me on Tuesday, having been delayed, I suppose, by the floods, which have been very bad on the lower road. Mr. Hadley's cart went into the ditch by the mill and they were two hours getting the horse out, though neither horse nor Hadley came to any harm beyond a wetting. We have had no post for three days before yours and I had begun to think the whole county had forgotten us.

I am glad to hear that the boys are well and that Tom has at last settled to his Latin. You must not let him bully you into letting him off; his father was just the same at that age and it did him no harm to be made to sit at the table until the exercise was done. Tell him his grandmother says so.

The hens have stopped laying, all but the speckled one, and Sarah says it is the cold. I say it is because she lets the dog in among them. We shall see who is right when the weather turns.

I send with this a small parcel of the quince jelly, which set very well this year, better than last, and some of the walnuts from the big tree, which are the last we shall have from it, as your father has decided it must come down before it takes the roof off the washhouse in a gale. I confess I shall be sorry to see it go.

Write again when you can, and do not wait for something to say. Your letters are the best thing that comes up the hill.

Your loving mother
